Procedure 1: Cataract Phacoemulsification & Foldable IOL (12 Steps)
STEP 1 Paracentesis Incision (~1.0mm MVR)
📁 cataract_01_paracentesis.mp3
🔊 EXACT SPOKEN SCRIPT (51 words • ~23s):
"Step 1: Make a small side door. Take the 1.0 millimeter blade from the left tray. Look for the flashing orange target at the top-left edge of the eye, at the 10 o'clock position. Click there to make a tiny slit. This gives your assistant tool a way into the eye."
Recommended Instrument: MVR BLADE
Target Location: Upper-left edge of the cornea at 10:00 o'clock (limbus border between clear cornea and white sclera).
Action Guide: 👉 Select the 1.0mm MVR Blade on the left tray, then CLICK the pulsing orange target at the 10 o'clock corneal edge.
Why Necessary: Eye surgery requires two hands. Your right hand holds the main ultrasound tool through the big incision, but you need a second tool (like a spatula or chopper) in your left hand to hold the cataract still, push pieces, and protect the back of the eye. Without this side door, you cannot control the cataract pieces and risk tearing the eye.
STEP 2 Tri-Planar Clear Corneal Incision (2.4mm)
📁 cataract_02_clear_corneal_incision.mp3
🔊 EXACT SPOKEN SCRIPT (56 words • ~25s):
"Step 2: Create your main doorway. Switch to the 2.4 millimeter keratome blade. Look for the flashing blue target at the upper-right, at the 1:30 o'clock position. Click 3 times to step through the three planes: groove the surface, tunnel through the wall, and enter the eye chamber. This creates a self-sealing tunnel that won't leak."
Recommended Instrument: KERATOME 2_4
Target Location: Upper-right corneal edge at 1:30 o'clock, where clear cornea transitions into the white of the eye.
Action Guide: 👉 Pick the 2.4mm Keratome blade. CLICK the pulsing blue target at the 1:30 o'clock position 3 times to complete Plane 1 (Groove), Plane 2 (Tunnel), and Plane 3 (AC Entry).
Why Necessary: Your primary ultrasound pen (phaco handpiece) is 2.4mm thick. You must create an entry tunnel wide enough for the pen to slide through easily, yet tight enough that fluid doesn't rush out and collapse the eye. Cutting it in 3 staggered planes creates an automatic valve: inside eye pressure presses the inner flap shut, keeping the eye pressurized and preventing bacteria from entering post-surgery.
STEP 3 OVD Injection (Dispersive & Cohesive)
📁 cataract_03_ovd_injection.mp3
🔊 EXACT SPOKEN SCRIPT (41 words • ~18s):
"Step 3: Protect the eye with jelly. Choose the Viscoat syringe on the left. Click inside the pupil to inject a clear protective gel. This coats the fragile inner lining of the cornea so ultrasonic soundwaves and turbulence won't damage it."
Recommended Instrument: OVD VISCOAT
Target Location: Center of the pupil and the dome directly behind the cornea (the anterior chamber).
Action Guide: 👉 Select Viscoat (dispersive jelly) or Provisc on the left tray, then CLICK inside the front chamber of the eye to inject the protective cushion.
Why Necessary: The back surface of your cornea is lined with fragile "endothelial cells" that act like microscopic bilge pumps keeping the cornea clear. Unlike skin or hair, these cells NEVER grow back once lost! If ultrasound vibrates near them or turbulent water washes over them, they die, turning the cornea permanently milky white. The viscous jelly acts like a protective suit of armor and keeps the eyeball round and firm.
STEP 4 Continuous Curvilinear Capsulorhexis (CCC)
📁 cataract_04_capsulorhexis.mp3
🔊 EXACT SPOKEN SCRIPT (55 words • ~24s):
"Step 4: Cut a circular window in the lens skin. Take the needle cystotome to poke a small tear in the center, then use the Utrata micro-forceps to steer the flap around the dashed blue circle. Make a smooth 5.2 millimeter circle. If it starts running wild toward the edge, pull back toward the center."
Recommended Instrument: UTRATA FORCEPS
Target Location: Center of the dark pupil, following the 5.2mm glowing blue circle guide.
Action Guide: 👉 1. Pick Cystotome and CLICK the center crosshair to puncture the skin. 2. Grab Utrata Forceps and DRAG in a smooth circle along the dashed blue guide ring.
Why Necessary: If you just randomly poke or shred the bag, sharp tears will zip down the sides to the back of the eye like a run in a nylon stocking! If that happens, the entire cataract falls into the back of the eyeball (vitreous) and you cannot put a replacement lens in place. A smooth, continuous circle distributes tension evenly and has immense structural strength.

Water cleaves these bonds safely with zero mechanical stress.

Recommended Instrument: IA HANDPIECE

Recommended Instrument: YAG LASER
Target Location: Cornea surface, centered over the pupil.
Action Guide: 👉 With the YAG Laser module active, CLICK on the eye to apply the Abraham contact lens.
Why Necessary: Months or years after cataract surgery, microscopic cells grow across the back bag like frosting on glass (a secondary cataract). We use an invisible infrared laser to blast a clear window through the haze. The Abraham contact lens widens the laser cone angle from 16° to 24°, concentrating energy strictly at the cloudy membrane while making it harmless to the cornea in front and the retina in the back.
STEP 2 Dual HeNe Aiming Beam Convergence
📁 yag_02_aiming_focus.mp3
🔊 EXACT SPOKEN SCRIPT (47 words • ~21s):
"Step 2: Align the twin red aiming lasers. Move your slit-lamp joystick forward or backward until the two separate red dots merge into a single crisp red point right on the cloudy membrane. When the two dots become one, you know the laser is perfectly in focus."
Recommended Instrument: YAG LASER
Target Location: On the opacified posterior capsule behind the artificial lens.
Action Guide: 👉 Move your cursor over the hazy membrane until the twin red aiming dots converge into a single tight red reticle.
Why Necessary: The therapeutic laser beam is 1064nm infrared—completely invisible to the human eye! To show you where it will strike, the machine projects two red aiming beams from different angles. When the two red spots merge into one, the invisible laser is focused with pinpoint accuracy. If they are separated, the laser will fire in the wrong depth plane!
STEP 3 Posterior Focal Offset (+150µm)
📁 yag_03_offset_adjustment.mp3
🔊 EXACT SPOKEN SCRIPT (46 words • ~20s):
"Step 3: Crucial safety setting! Set the posterior offset to plus 150 micrometers. Never fire at zero offset! When the laser sparks, the mini shockwave expands forward. If you don't set a safety gap, the shockwave will pit, scratch, and crack the patient's expensive artificial lens!"
Recommended Instrument: YAG LASER
Target Location: The right machine console: "Defocus Offset" dial set to +150 µm.
Action Guide: 👉 Verify the Laser Defocus Offset is set between +150µm and +200µm in the right console panel.
Why Necessary: This is the number one rookie mistake in ophthalmology! When a laser spark creates plasma, the plasma sparks and expands FORWARD back toward the incoming laser beam. If your focus is set directly on the capsule (zero offset), the shockwave strikes the posterior surface of the plastic lens, blasting permanent micro-craters (pits) into the optic, creating glare, halos, and permanent vision distortion for the patient.

Because the drainage angle of the eye is hidden around the side curve behind the cornea, light from straight above cannot see it. Tilt the microscope 35 to 40 degrees towards yourself, and tilt the patient's head 30 to 35 degrees away. Click the alignment beacon to set optimal direct gonioscopic optical trajectory."
Recommended Instrument: NONE
Target Location: Microscope ocular axis and patient cervical rotation angle.
Action Guide: 👉 CLICK the Tilt Alignment control on the angle HUD to align the microscope and patient head to 38°/35°.
Why Necessary: Normal light from a surgical microscope shines straight down into the pupil. But the eye's microscopic drainage canal (Schlemm's canal) is tucked sideways around the 360-degree perimeter of the corneal rim! If you look straight down, total internal reflection inside the curved cornea completely hides the drain! Tilting the microscope and head lets you peek sideways into the drainage angle.

And because the blood pressure will always push back at 8 to 10 mmHg, the patient's eye pressure can never drop too low (protecting against hypotony)!
